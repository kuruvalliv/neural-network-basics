§5.1 – §5.2 · Concept 1

Genetic vs behavioral diversity

Evolution naturally loses diversity: the population piles onto the best peak. That is useful for refining, but deadly if it happens too early (premature convergence). The fix starts with a question: different in what way?

Genomes can be far apart yet behave identically, or nearly identical yet behave in opposite ways. In the demo, each line is the path of a small robot steered by a 3-neuron network. Try the two buttons on the highlighted robot.

Swapping neurons is competing conventions: big genome jump, zero behavior change. Negating outputs is a small edit that mirrors the path. That is why genetic distance is a poor proxy for behavioral distance (§5.2).

Cluster: tools for keeping genetic diversity (§5.1)

  • Inject variation
    • raise mutation rate (random material, hurts if too high)
    • delta-coding: restart from Δ-offsets around the champion
  • Slow the takeover
    • crowding: newcomers replace similar individuals
    • fitness sharing: divide fitness by "how crowded you are"
    • implicit sharing in cooperative coevolution (§7.1)
  • Remember the past
    • archive of representative individuals
f′(x) = f(x)s(x),   s(x) = nΣj=1 d(x, yj)
fitness sharing, eq. 5.1–5.2
f′(x)The shared fitness that selection actually sees.
f(x)Raw task fitness of individual x.
s(x)Crowding count: sum over all n members yj. Bigger when many others are similar.
d(x,yj)A similarity contribution. In practice it is a sharing kernel that is large when x and yj are close (e.g. 1 − dist/σ inside radius σ, else 0), so crowded individuals get a big divisor.

Cost: this is O(n²) per generation; the book notes it can be limited to a local neighborhood or a sample.

Behavior characterization (BC)

To measure behavioral diversity you first turn behavior into a vector, the BC (also called behavior descriptor). Then reuse the same distance tricks. The options cluster into three families:

  • Hand-designed
    • final position of a robot, or histograms of sensors/actions/locations
    • which lights are on, as a bit string like 1001000 (Mouret & Doncieux light task)
  • Probe-based
    • outputs on a fixed set of inputs (random if no domain knowledge)
    • outputs at key decision points; action distributions for recurrent nets
  • Learned embedding tie
    • meta-learned BCs across related tasks (Meyerson et al.)
    • AURORA: PCA or autoencoder over sensor data, latent = BC (§5.4.4)
Entropy (population level)
Bin the behavior space, count agents per bin; high entropy of that histogram = diverse population.
Empowerment, E = maxp(at) I(St+1; At) (eq. 5.3)
At actions now, St+1 sensor readings next step, I mutual information (eq. 5.4: H(S) − H(S|A), how much of what you sense is explained by what you did), max over p(at) pick the action policy that makes this largest. Plain words: how much the agent can steer its own future senses. Not a diversity measure, but it tends to diversify actions.
Why it matters for your apps: a sentence embedding of an agent's output, or of the stdout/test results of an evolved program, is a learned BC for free. Choose an embedding aligned with what "useful difference" means (§5.4.4 shows misaligned BCs hurt).
Check: two genomes have L2 distance 9.4 but identical trajectories. How?

Competing conventions: the same hidden neurons are stored in a different order. The function is unchanged, so behavioral distance is 0.

Check: in the lights task, why does fitness alone fail?

Fitness is only given when light 6 turns on, so almost every robot scores zero: the landscape is flat. A BC of "which lights are on" rewards new light patterns and builds stepping stones toward light 6.

§5.3 · Concept 2

The novelty score

Novelty search throws away the task objective. An individual scores high if its behavior lies in an empty region of behavior space, compared with the current population plus an archive of past behaviors. Search becomes divergent.

Why it matters for your apps: novelty is literally a kNN query. With a vector index over embeddings of past outputs (prompts, plans, programs), mean(top_k_distances) gives a novelty bonus you can add to an LLM-evolution selection step.
Skip-ahead Picbreeder (§8.3) is the origin story of "stepping stones". Read §5.3 here first, then come back to it after Chapter 8 for the full picture.
Check: what happens with no archive at all?

Novelty is measured only against the current population, so the search can drift back into regions it already explored generations ago. It cycles instead of diverging.

Check: why can pure novelty still find good solutions?

To keep being different, it pays to exploit structure in the domain. Search finds stepping stones (e.g. falling, jumping) that combine into better solutions, like robust walking gaits in the biped example (§5.3).

§5.2 – §5.3 · Concept 3

Deception: the maze test

A fitness landscape is deceptive when the path to the best peak goes through a valley. The classic novelty-search test (Lehman & Stanley) is a maze where "closer to the goal" leads into a dead end.

Both panels run the same GA on the same robot. Genome: 12 headings, each driven for 5 steps; walls block movement. Left selects by −distance to goal. Right selects by novelty of the end point (BC = final x,y; k = 10, plus archive).

In offline tests of this exact setup (20 runs, 300 generations), novelty reached the goal in 20/20 runs (median ~40 generations) and fitness in about 4/20. Your run can differ; press Reset a few times.

Why it matters for your apps: a code-evolution loop scored only by "tests passed" is deceptive in the same way: the right refactor often breaks tests first. Rewarding novel behavior keeps those intermediate programs alive.
Check: where do the fitness robots pile up, and why?

Below the wall, closest to the goal in straight-line distance. Every step toward the real exit (right side) lowers fitness, so selection never keeps it.

§5.4 · Concept 4

Quality diversity: MAP-Elites

Quality diversity (QD) wants a whole collection: the best solution for every kind of behavior. This "illuminates" the space: you see how performance varies across behaviors.

Cluster: QD at a glance

  • Implicit niches: NSLC §5.4.2
    • objective 1: novelty (eq. 5.5, kNN in morphology space)
    • objective 2: local competition = how many of my k neighbors I beat
    • combined with NSGA-II (multiobjective)
  • Explicit niches: MAP-Elites §5.4.3
    • user grid over BC dimensions
    • each cell keeps one elite (best so far)
  • Enhancements §5.4.4
    • BC choice: aligned beats misaligned; learned BCs (meta-learning, AURORA)
    • Niches: CVT-MAP-Elites (k-means centroids, scales to ~1,000 dims)
    • Optimizer: ME-ES, CMA-ME, CMA-MEGA (gradients), CMA-MAE (annealed acceptance)

Check: you add a third BC dimension at resolution 20. How many cells?

20³ = 8,000 instead of 400. This exponential growth is the curse of dimensionality that CVT-MAP-Elites avoids by using a fixed number of centroids.

Check: why is MAP-Elites' insertion O(1) but CVT's O(k)?

A grid cell index is computed directly from the BC. CVT must find the nearest of k centroids, which needs distance checks (worst case all k).

§5.5 · Concept 5

Multiobjectivity

With several objectives there are many ways to be good, so diversity comes almost for free. The result is a Pareto front: solutions where improving one objective must worsen another. You pick one later, using deployment conditions or human taste.

Demo: NSGA-II style selection (non-dominated rank, then crowding distance) on the ZDT1 benchmark, minimizing both f1 and f2. The dashed curve is the true front.

dominatesA dominates B if A is no worse on every objective and strictly better on one.
front (rank 0)Nobody dominates them. Highlighted in teal.
w sliderPicks the front member minimizing w·f1 + (1−w)·f2: a stand-in for "choose by preference after the search".

The book also mentions NEWS/D: decompose a behavior into several single-objective problems; the non-specialized front members transferred best to new tasks.

Why it matters for your apps: for an embedding-driven decision system, keep a front over (accuracy, latency, cost) rather than one "best" model, and select at run time.
Check: why might "survival of the species" be a poor single objective in practice?

It allows creativity, but the signal is weak: good partial ideas can't be recognized until they're fully developed. Multiple concrete objectives sit one level below and give usable gradients.

§5.6 · Concept 6

Ensembling

Evolution ends with a population, not a single model. If members are diverse, combining them (voting, averaging, gating) beats any one of them. Without it, says the book, an experiment "leaves money on the table".

Cluster: ensembling flavors in the book

  • Combine outputs
    • voting, averaging
    • species champions from NEAT (§3.3)
  • Choose a member
    • gating network (telescoping-pole "fly swatter": 8 champions + evolved gate)
    • confidence bets: pick the most confident net
  • Built-in ensembles
    • ESP subpopulations (§7.1.1), hierarchical teams
    • MM-NEAT modules with preference neurons (§6.3), context+skill (§6.2)
Why it matters for your apps: diversity, not size, is the lever. Three LLM-generated programs that fail on different inputs, plus a gate, beat ten near-copies.
Check: set diversity to 0. What happens as M grows, and why?

Accuracy stays at p. All members copy the same call, so voting adds nothing.

§5.7 · Concept 7

Population culture and history

Culture = knowledge spread across the population: shared behaviors, their variants, and individual quirks. A cheap way to read it is a syllabus: a fixed set of probe inputs given to everyone.

  • Within a generation
    • Culling: make a large litter, discard offspring whose syllabus answers stray far from the parents' (most crossovers are awful). ~3× faster in pole balancing.
    • Mate selection: second parent = competent but as different as possible (better offspring 15% vs 7% in acrobot).
    • Discarding: remove the weaker of the most similar pair. 3× diversity, 30% faster.
  • Learning from culture see §4.2.3 Baldwin
    • train offspring on parents' syllabus answers (Baldwin effect)
    • Egalitarian social learning: anyone can teach anyone in situations where they earned more reward; subcultures keep diversity
  • Models of the population
    • EDAs, eugenic neuroevolution: build offspring from fitness statistics
  • History
    • Neuroannealing: keep every solution ever seen in a 4-level partition tree; pick parents via Boltzmann sampling

Demo of culling: two good parents encode the same function with hidden units in a different order. Crossover by position mixes mismatched units, so many children are terrible. We score each child only on a 6-input syllabus vs the parents, keep the closest fraction, and then reveal their true error.

Why it matters for your apps: before running an expensive test suite on 200 LLM-proposed patches, run a tiny syllabus of inputs and cull the ones whose outputs (or output embeddings) are wildly off from the parent. Cheap, and it removes mostly the non-viable ones.
Check: egalitarian vs student-teacher, what's the key difference?

Student-teacher copies a few champions, shrinking diversity. Egalitarian learning lets any agent learn from any other in the specific situations where that other did better, so niche skills spread without everyone collapsing onto one strategy.

Review questions (paraphrased from §5.8)

  • Foundations
    • Why does diversity matter both in nature and in neuroevolution?

      It allows complex solutions to be built over time from existing pieces and lets populations adapt when the environment changes.

    • What goes wrong when a population converges too fast?

      Recombination of near-identical parents creates nothing new; only mutation is left, which is close to random search, and dynamic tasks can't be tracked.

    • Fitness sharing vs crowding?

      Sharing divides fitness by local crowdedness (costly O(n²)); crowding makes newcomers replace similar individuals. Both slow convergence rather than create diversity.

  • Behavior and search
    • Define a BC for robot navigation.

      For example the final (x, y), or positions sampled along the trajectory, or a histogram of visited locations.

    • How does QD differ from plain optimization?

      It returns a collection: the best solution for each region of behavior space, maintained via local competition (NSLC) or per-cell elites (MAP-Elites).

    • How does multiobjectivity boost diversity?

      There are many ways to succeed; the Pareto front holds distinct trade-offs and niches emerge naturally.

  • Harnessing
    • Why do EAs suit ensembling?

      They already produce a diverse population; NEAT species champions can be combined with voting, averaging or a gate.

    • What is egalitarian social learning?

      Every agent can learn from every other agent's action in situations where that agent got more reward; subcultures preserve diversity.

§2.1 · Concept 1

The evolutionary loop

One job: see that every EA is the same four-beat cycle.

An evolutionary algorithm (EA) keeps a population, scores each member with a fitness function, lets the better ones breed, and repeats (§2.1, fig. 2.2). No error gradient is required, only a way to tell good from bad.

The book frames this with the solver pattern: ask() hands you candidates, you score them, tell() hands the scores back.

while True:
    xs = solver.ask()                     # candidates for this generation
    fs = [evaluate(x) for x in xs]      # fitness, any black box
    solver.tell(fs)                       # the algorithm updates itself
    if solver.result()[1] > TARGET: break

For your appsIn an LLM code-evolution app the genotype is source code, the phenotype is the running program, the LLM edit is the mutation operator, and your test suite is the fitness function. Same loop.
Why can very high selection pressure hurt, even though it converges faster?
It discards almost everyone but the current leaders, so diversity collapses. If the leaders sit on a local hill, nobody is left exploring elsewhere: premature convergence.
For an evolved prompt, what is the genotype and what is the phenotype?
Genotype: the prompt text (or the template parameters) that gets edited. Phenotype: the model's behavior when run with that prompt, which is what your evaluator actually scores.
§2.2.2 · Concept 2

Simple Gaussian ES

One job: sample a cloud around a center, jump the center to the winner.

Evolution strategies (ES) work on real-valued vectors. The simplest one in the book keeps a single center m, sprinkles λ offspring around it with Gaussian noise of fixed width σ, and moves the center to the best offspring. Fast and greedy, so it stalls on rugged ground.

offspringmean mbest so far

0.50

Try σ = 0.2 on Rastrigin: the center hops to the nearest bump and freezes. σ = 1.2 escapes but never settles precisely.

Two canonical survivor rules

  • (μ, λ)-ES only the λ children compete; parents always die
  • (μ + λ)-ES parents and children compete together; a good parent can live on

μ is how many parents survive, λ how many children are made (usually λ ≥ μ). The book's worked example uses μ = 1.

For your appsA (1 + λ) loop is the honest baseline for code evolution: ask the LLM for λ edits, keep the best if it beats the parent. Beat this before building anything fancier.
Why does simple ES get stuck on Rastrigin when σ is small?
Every sample lands on the same local bump, and the best of them is that bump's top. It throws away all other information, so nothing pulls it toward the global peak.
§2.2.3 · Concept 3

CMA-ES: learn the shape of the cloud

One job: let the winners decide where the cloud goes and how it is stretched.

Fixed σ is the weakness of simple ES. Sometimes you want to search wide, sometimes to fine-tune. CMA-ES keeps a full Gaussian and refits it each generation from the top performers. Three entities to track:

  • Mean μ where the cloud sits
  • Covariance C its shape: stretch and tilt
    • step size its overall size, which here lives inside C

Where is step-size adaptation?

In this simplified version it is automatic. If the elites land far from the old mean (you are on a slope), the spread measured from μ(g) is large, so C grows. If the elites huddle around the old mean (you are near a peak), C shrinks. Watch size in the readout. Full CMA-ES blends each new C with the old one and adds a separate global step size driven by "evolution paths" beyond this chapter (Hansen, 2016).

Skip-aheadIf covariance feels abstract, skip the equations on a first pass: click "Next phase" ten times on the tilted valley and watch the ellipse rotate to match it. Then come back to the table.

Cost. Updating C is O(N²) in the number of parameters N. The authors' rule of thumb: great below about 1,000 parameters, usable up to around 10,000 with patience.

For your appsTuning a few dozen continuous knobs of an embedding-based decision system (similarity thresholds, feature weights, temperature) is CMA-ES's sweet spot. Too few parameters for a neural net, too coupled for grid search.
On the tilted valley, why does the ellipse rotate?
Elites all lie along the valley's diagonal, so their x and y deviations share a sign. That makes σxy large, which tilts C along the valley, so the next samples are spent where good solutions actually are.
What happens to C once the mean sits on the optimum?
The elites are now the samples closest to the mean, so their spread from the old mean is smaller than the cloud's. C shrinks generation by generation: the search automatically fine-tunes.
§2.2.4 · Concept 4

OpenAI-ES: a gradient from noise

One job: average random probes, weighted by how well they did, to get an uphill direction.

OpenAI-ES (Salimans et al., 2017) drops the covariance matrix. It keeps a parameter vector θ, pokes it in N random directions, and adds up the directions weighted by their scores. The sum approximates the gradient, which then feeds an ordinary optimizer such as Adam.

  • θ the single current solution (the mean)
  • εi random probe directions
    • σ how far each probe reaches
  • F score of each probe
    • fitness shaping use ranks, not raw scores
  • α, Adam how big a step to take uphill

Three engineering tricks

  • Fitness shaping (§2.2.4, fig. 2.10)
    • replace scores by ranks so one outlier cannot hijack the gradient. Untick "rank shaping" on Schaffer to see the raw sum wobble.
  • Shared random seeds
    • every worker can regenerate every other worker's ε, so each sends back a single number, its fitness. That is what scales to thousands of machines.
  • Antithetic pairs not in the chapter text
    • probe both θ + σε and θ − σε. Common in the original implementation; it cancels the constant part of F and lowers noise.
Skip-aheadAdam is a gradient-descent detail. If it is unfamiliar, read it as "a smarter step" and return after skimming §2.3.2. The real payoff of OpenAI-ES (training RL policies) comes in §3.4.2.
For your appsWhen the reward is a click, a passed test or a user rating, there is no derivative. OpenAI-ES only needs those scores, and scales to millions of network weights.
If every probe scores the same, what does eq. 2.10 return?
With raw scores the sum becomes F·Σεi, which averages out to about zero (exactly zero with antithetic pairs). No signal, no step: a flat region gives no direction. Ranks would only add tie-breaking noise.

Things to try. Tilted valley: CMA-ES's ellipse turns to lie along the valley, then shrinks as it closes in. Rastrigin, σ = 0.3: simple ES often freezes on a local bump, and CMA-ES collapses even faster: this simplified version shrinks C whenever the elites huddle, which is premature convergence in action. Raise λ to 100 and σ to 1.5 and CMA-ES usually finds the global peak. Rastrigin, σ = 1.2: OpenAI-ES glides over the bumps because a wide probe averages them out. In 2D all three often get close; the book's 100-D Rastrigin test (§2.2.7) is where CMA-ES pulled clearly ahead, and OpenAI-ES needed σ slowly lowered (annealed).

Picking one (§2.2.7)

  • Few parameters (< ~1k), coupled
    • CMA-ES
  • Huge parameter count, lots of parallel compute, noisy reward
    • OpenAI-ES
  • Not purely continuous, custom operators
    • GA
Why can OpenAI-ES cross Rastrigin's bumps when simple ES cannot, at the same σ?
Simple ES jumps to a single best sample, so one lucky local bump captures it. OpenAI-ES averages all probes into one direction and takes a smoothed (momentum) step, so it follows the overall bowl shape.
§2.2.5 · Concept 6

Many goals: the Pareto front and NSGA-II

One job: when goals conflict, find the whole menu of best trade-offs.

Cost vs. performance, speed vs. accuracy: often no single answer wins on everything. So the output is a set of trade-offs. Entities first:

  • Dominance A beats B if A is no worse on every objective and strictly better on one
    • Pareto-optimal nobody dominates it
    • Pareto front the set of all Pareto-optimal outcomes
  • Goals of a good approximation
    • convergence close to the true front
    • diversity spread along it
  • NSGA-II's three mechanisms
    • keep: elitism merge parents + children (2N), keep best N
    • rank: fast non-dominated sorting front 1, remove, front 2, ...
    • spread: crowding distance ties go to lonely points
front 1front 2front 3+weighted-sum pick

Skip-aheadCrowding distance matters most when diversity becomes the goal itself. Skim it now; it returns in §5.5 (diversity) and the multiobjective sections of Ch 6, 10 and 14.
For your appsCode evolution has natural objectives: tests passed, runtime, code length. A decision system has accuracy vs. inference cost. Keep the front, then let a person pick the trade-off.
NSGA-II needs 40 survivors; fronts 1–2 hold 30, front 3 holds 18. Who gets the last 10 slots?
The 10 members of front 3 with the largest crowding distance, i.e. those in the emptiest stretches of that front.
Why does NSGA-II work only up to about half a dozen objectives?
With many objectives almost nobody dominates anybody, so nearly everyone sits on front 1 and ranking loses its pressure. NSGA-III and many-objective methods address this.

Bridge: a neural network as the genome

One job: see that a network's weights are just a vector, so any EA above can evolve them.

A feedforward network turns inputs into outputs through layers of weighted sums and nonlinear activation functions. Flatten all its weights and biases into one list and you have a genotype. That is neuroevolution in one sentence (§2.4).

For your appsPut an embedding in as x and a decision out as y, and you can evolve the decision head directly on a business metric that has no gradient.
Why does this XOR-like layout need a hidden layer with a nonlinearity?
No single straight line separates opposite quadrants. Hidden tanh units each draw a line; the output combines them into a non-linear region. Without the nonlinearity, layers collapse into one line.

Review questions (paraphrased from §2.5)

Name the core parts of an EA and how together they mimic natural selection.
Representation and population (variation exists), fitness and selection (the fitter reproduce more), mutation and crossover (heritable change), replacement and termination (generations turn over). Repeated, this is descent with modification under selection.
In a GA, what do crossover and mutation each contribute to diversity?
Crossover recombines existing good building blocks from two parents; mutation injects genuinely new values and keeps the population from collapsing to one point.
How does CMA-ES adapt its search, and why is that better than simple ES?
It refits mean and covariance from the top samples each generation, so the cloud stretches along promising directions, grows on slopes and shrinks near optima. Simple ES has one fixed σ and can only be either exploratory or precise.
What is hard about multiobjective EAs, and how does NSGA-II respond?
You must approximate a whole front, both close to optimal and well spread. NSGA-II keeps elites from a 2N merge, ranks by non-dominated fronts, and breaks ties with crowding distance.
What is rank-based fitness shaping and why does it help?
Replace raw scores with their ranks (centered, e.g. in [−0.5, 0.5]). One huge outlier then counts no more than the second-best, so it cannot dominate the gradient estimate or pull the search into a trap.
Why do hidden layers need a nonlinear activation?
A stack of linear layers is just one linear map. The nonlinearity lets the network bend decision boundaries and model complex input-output relations.
